2  Wellness, Stress, and Resilience

2.1 Introduction

University life can be an exciting opportunity for learning, personal growth, and new experiences. It can also involve competing responsibilities, academic expectations, financial pressures, changing relationships, and uncertainty about the future.

NURS 180 introduces students to foundational knowledge about stress, stressors, resilience, social support, and strategies for promoting physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual well-being.

This chapter introduces wellness, stress, resilience, and the experience of transitioning to university. It provides a foundation for exploring practical approaches to managing stress throughout the course.

2.2 Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the concepts of wellness, stress, stressors, and resilience.
  2. Identify common stressors experienced by university students.
  3. Describe how stress can be experienced differently by different individuals.
  4. Reflect on personal responses to stressful situations.
  5. Identify initial strategies and sources of support that may help them navigate student life.

2.3 1.1 Understanding wellness

Wellness can be understood as an ongoing process of recognizing personal needs and developing practices that support well-being.

Well-being is not simply the absence of illness or difficulty. Students may experience challenges while also finding meaning, maintaining relationships, learning new skills, and developing strategies to care for themselves.

In this course, wellness includes physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual dimensions of health. These dimensions are interconnected and can be influenced by personal circumstances, relationships, and the wider social environment.

For example, a student experiencing academic pressure may notice changes in sleep, feel worried about an upcoming assignment, and have less time to connect with friends. Considering these experiences together can help the student identify what support or changes may be useful.

2.4 1.2 Understanding stress and stressors

Stress refers to the experience of responding to demands, challenges, or situations that a person perceives as requiring attention or adaptation.

A stressor is a situation, event, demand, or circumstance that contributes to a stress response.

Stressors may be related to academic responsibilities, finances, family expectations, relationships, health, work, or uncertainty about the future.

Stress is not always experienced in the same way. A situation that feels manageable to one student may feel overwhelming to another. Previous experiences, available resources, social support, and the meaning attached to a situation may influence how a person responds.

2.4.1 Common student stressors

Examples include:

  • Managing multiple assignments and deadlines.
  • Preparing for examinations.
  • Adjusting to university expectations and new learning environments.
  • Balancing employment, studies, family, and personal responsibilities.
  • Experiencing financial uncertainty.
  • Navigating relationships, belonging, and social expectations.
  • Managing uncertainty about academic progress or future career plans.

This list is not exhaustive. Students may experience several stressors at once, and their circumstances may change throughout the academic year.

2.4.2 Reflection: Identifying stressors

Consider the following questions:

  1. What situations have contributed to stress in your academic or personal life?
  2. Which stressors are within your control, partly within your control, or outside your immediate control?
  3. What resources, relationships, or skills have helped you respond to these situations?

You do not need to share private experiences with others. Choose an example that you feel comfortable reflecting on.

2.5 1.3 Thriving or surviving?

The distinction between thriving and surviving provides a useful starting point for reflecting on student well-being.

Surviving may describe periods when a person is concentrating mainly on getting through immediate demands. A student may complete assignments and attend classes while feeling that there is little time or energy left for rest, relationships, or activities they value.

Thriving may describe a greater sense of engagement, connection, purpose, and capacity to manage challenges. Thriving does not mean feeling happy or stress-free at all times.

These descriptions are not fixed categories. A student may feel that they are thriving in one area of life while struggling in another. A difficult week does not necessarily mean that a person is failing, and experiencing stress does not mean that well-being is impossible.

The purpose of this reflection is to recognize current needs and identify realistic opportunities for improvement.

2.5.1 Reflection activity: Where am I now?

Think about your current experience of university life.

  • What is going well for you?
  • What feels particularly demanding?
  • Are you making time for sleep, nourishment, movement, relationships, and activities that matter to you?
  • What is one manageable change that might support your well-being this week?

Record your reflections privately if you prefer.

2.6 1.4 Understanding resilience

Resilience refers to the capacity and process of adapting to challenges, responding to difficulties, and drawing on available resources.

Resilience should not be understood as having to cope with everything alone. Asking for help, taking a break, adjusting expectations, and seeking appropriate support can all be part of responding constructively to difficult circumstances.

Resilience can involve personal skills, relationships, community resources, and the conditions in which a person lives and studies. It is therefore important to consider both individual strategies and the availability of supportive environments.

For example, a student who is struggling with a demanding workload might review their schedule, speak with an instructor, seek academic support, and ask a trusted person for help. These actions may help the student respond to the situation more effectively than trying to manage every difficulty alone.

2.6.1 Building resilience through reflection

Consider the following questions:

  • How have you responded to a difficult situation in the past?
  • What helped you manage that experience?
  • What did you learn about your needs, strengths, or limits?
  • What would you do differently if a similar situation occurred again?
  • Who or what could support you during a future challenge?

Resilience does not require a person to tolerate unsafe, unfair, or persistently harmful circumstances. Sometimes, responding to a challenge requires changing the situation or seeking support rather than simply adapting to it.

2.7 1.5 Developing an initial stress-management approach

Managing stress begins with recognizing what is happening and considering what kind of response may be appropriate.

A useful starting process is:

  1. Notice: Identify the situation contributing to stress.
  2. Reflect: Consider how the situation is affecting you and what you need.
  3. Prioritize: Identify what requires immediate attention and what can wait.
  4. Choose: Select one realistic action, such as breaking a task into smaller steps or contacting a support person.
  5. Review: Reflect on whether the action helped and whether another response is needed.

This is a practical reflection framework for the course, not a guarantee that every stressful situation can be resolved through individual action.

Different situations call for different responses. Some may benefit from planning and problem-solving, while others may require emotional support, rest, professional assistance, or changes to external circumstances.

2.8 1.6 Academic stress and the transition to university

The transition to university may involve changes in learning expectations, independence, time management, relationships, and personal responsibilities.

Students may need to develop new approaches to studying, seek clarification when expectations are unclear, and learn how to balance academic work with other aspects of life.

Academic integrity is also part of navigating university successfully. Understanding course expectations, acknowledging sources, managing deadlines, and seeking guidance when uncertain can help students approach academic responsibilities constructively.

Students are encouraged to identify support resources available through their institution, including academic advising, learning support, student wellness services, and trusted instructors or peers.

Seeking assistance is not evidence of personal failure. It can be a practical way of responding to a challenge.

2.9 Chapter summary

In this chapter, we explored wellness, stress, stressors, resilience, and the experience of university life.

Key points include:

  • Wellness involves several interconnected dimensions of health and well-being.
  • Stressors may arise from academic, financial, social, personal, and other circumstances.
  • People may experience and respond to similar situations differently.
  • Thriving and surviving are useful concepts for reflecting on current needs, not permanent labels.
  • Resilience can involve personal skills, supportive relationships, and access to resources.
  • Recognizing stressors and choosing realistic next steps can provide a starting point for stress management.
  • Seeking help can be an important part of navigating challenges.

2.10 Review questions

  1. What is the difference between stress and a stressor?
  2. Identify three stressors that university students may experience.
  3. How might two students respond differently to the same stressful situation?
  4. What is resilience, and why does it not mean coping alone?
  5. How might a student recognize that they are surviving rather than thriving?
  6. Identify one realistic action that could help a student respond to an academic stressor.
  7. What forms of support might be available to a student experiencing persistent stress?
